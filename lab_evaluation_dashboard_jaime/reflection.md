# Reflection

**How I applied the communication-layer principles.** Every chart title is a question or conclusion a manager cares about ("Where the model is weakest…", "How scores are spread…") rather than a field name. I added two plain-language columns before importing: skill names ("Following instructions" instead of `instruction_following`) and score bands ("Poor 0–40" … "Excellent 80–100"), so nobody has to interpret a histogram bin. Three headline numbers sit at the top (average score, share of poor scores, number of evaluations), and a footer says in two sentences what the data means and what to do about it: reasoning is the weak spot, and the four models are within about 2 points of each other, so changing model will not solve it. Colours carry meaning (red = poor, dark blue = excellent).

**What I included and excluded.** Included: averages, counts, shares, filters for model, skill and date, and cross-filtering by clicking a bar. Excluded on purpose: standard deviations, quartiles, box plots, p-values, confidence intervals and any statistical test. Those stay in the analysis layer (the statistics lab and notebooks); a box plot, for example, is precise but most executives cannot read it. **Next time** I would use real evaluation data from a larger run, show the trend over time for the weakest skill, and fix two details I could not finish: the date slicer shows "no filters applied" instead of the date range, and Power BI's automatic subtitles mix Spanish ("por") into an English dashboard because the application runs in Spanish.
